Introduction to Digital Forensics

What digital forensics actually is, why it matters far beyond "hacking" cases, and the one rule every investigator follows before touching a single file.

8 min read No prior experience needed Part of our Digital Forensics series
Browse our courses Jump to the five domains

What is digital forensics?

Digital forensics is the practice of identifying, preserving, examining, and presenting digital evidence in a way that holds up to legal or organizational scrutiny. It's less about "hacking into" devices and more about disciplined, repeatable process — every step has to be defensible, because the whole point is that someone else, a court, an auditor, an opposing lawyer, will eventually check your work.

It sits at the intersection of three skill sets: technical (understanding how computers and storage actually work), procedural (following a chain of custody so evidence can't be challenged), and communicative (explaining technical findings to non-technical people — judges, juries, executives).

Why it matters

Digital evidence now shows up in almost every kind of investigation, not just "cybercrime" in the narrow sense. A fraud investigation needs transaction logs and deleted spreadsheets. A wrongful termination dispute needs email timestamps. An IP-theft case needs to prove which files were copied to a USB drive, and when. A ransomware incident response needs to know how attackers got in and what they touched.

In each case, the same core skill applies: recover the truth from digital systems without corrupting it in the process.

The golden rule

REMEMBER THIS

Never work on the original evidence.

Every action you take on a live system, even just opening a file to look at it, can change timestamps or overwrite data that might matter later. The standard practice is to create a forensic image (a bit-for-bit copy) of the original media first, verify that copy matches the original using a cryptographic hash, and then do all analysis on the copy — never the original again.

A simple scenario

EXAMPLE

Imagine a company suspects an employee copied confidential client files to a personal USB drive before resigning. An untrained response would be to immediately open the employee's laptop and start browsing folders — which risks altering file access timestamps and destroying the very evidence needed to prove or disprove the claim. A trained response images the laptop's drive first, then examines the image for USB connection history, file access logs, and recently modified files, all without ever touching the original machine again. That difference is the entire value a forensics-trained investigator brings.

Career paths

Digital forensics skills are used in law enforcement and government cyber units, corporate security and incident response teams, legal and eDiscovery support roles, and independent consulting for smaller firms that need occasional expert support. Many practitioners enter through IT or cybersecurity roles and specialize into forensics over time, rather than starting there directly.
